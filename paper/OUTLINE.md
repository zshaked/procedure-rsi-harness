# Paper outline

Working title: **Learn to Verify Before You Act? Expert-Time Allocation for Latent Operational Procedures**

Status: outline v0.2, revised after external review (commit 0c01a49). No experiments run. The two-week pilot in §9 runs before anything else and decides whether the method claim survives.

Companion documents: `MATH.md` (formal statements, with their limits), `../benchmark/README.md` (testbed), `../DESIGN.md` and `../docs/` (the harness).

---

## 1. The question

> Under a fixed expert-time budget, when should we improve the specification, the verifier, or the agent directly — and how does that choice affect independently measured correctness?

Framing that must survive into the abstract: **the bottleneck is specification, not evaluator intelligence.** A frontier model can judge; it cannot know what "correct" means on a desk whose procedure was never written down. The paper measures which use of scarce expert time acquires that specification most cheaply, and whether a learned verifier earns its complexity over simply handing the inferred specification to the agent.

The earlier framing ("when can a learned verifier stand in for the human") is retained as one measurement (§4, secondary), not as the thesis, because the review correctly noted that the guarantee machinery is not yet sound and that the verifier's advantage over a spec-in-context policy is unproven.

---

## 2. Draft abstract

> Operational procedures in enterprises are rarely written down. What exists is a history of requests, the responses humans sent, and the state of the system of record at the time — and a human who can say whether a response is acceptable. Self-improvement methods for agent harnesses assume either a ground-truth verifier or none at all; the common enterprise case is neither: correctness is partly implicit in imperfect historical behavior and partly held by operators whose time is scarce, and optimization can exploit mistakes in whatever is learned from them. We argue the bottleneck is specification, not evaluator intelligence, and ask how a fixed expert-time budget should be split between inferring the specification, calibrating a verifier, and improving the agent directly. We release [BENCHMARK]: outcome-only histories generated by hidden executable policies, with operator inconsistency, multiple held-out policy instances, and a budgeted noisy oracle, so that verifier exploitation can be measured against ground truth independent of any model's opinion. Under matched histories, expert seconds and compute, we find [that verifier-first search beats handing the inferred specification to the agent only when …]; [that checklist verifiers with provenance reach usable agreement with fewer labels and are exploited less than holistic or exemplar-based verifiers]; and [that search concentrates on the verifier's errors in a way predicted by where those errors fall]. [Aggregate results from a production deployment reproduce the benchmark's curves.]

Bracketed claims are hypotheses; each is replaced by the measured result or deleted.

## 3. Contributions and novelty, stated narrowly

The review's overlap table is adopted as the honest starting point:

| Ingredient we use | Closest prior work | Our relation to it |
|---|---|---|
| Reflective procedure / harness evolution | Procedural Graphs, ACE, GEPA, Harness², RRSI | Used as machinery; not a contribution |
| Rubric learned from human-scored examples | Learnable Assessment Skills (2605.29274) | Baseline for H2 |
| Rubric memory alternating with policy improvement under a small expert budget | **SibylSense** (2602.20751); EvoRubrics (2606.23038); AdaRubric (2603.21362) | Closest prior method. Direct comparison required. Differences: they train policy weights for open-ended QA with a reference answer; we evolve a procedure against replayable state with no reference and no written spec |
| Selective judging with human-agreement guarantees | Trust or Escalate (2024) | Adopted, not claimed; our stand-in measurement uses their construction |
| Locking label-derived rubric checks against drift | Rulers / Locked Rubrics (2601.08654); Rubrics as an Attack Surface (2602.13576) | Adopted for provenance tiers (H4 is an ablation against these, not a new idea) |

What remains as contribution:

1. **Setting.** Hidden operational rules, outcome-only histories, imperfect historical responses, scarce expert feedback, and evaluation *after* policy optimization against the learned verifier — the combination is not studied, and the enterprise case is common.
2. **Benchmark.** Executable hidden policies (ground truth independent of any LLM), multiple policy instances with policy holdout, operator inconsistency, outcome-only histories, budgeted noisy oracle, history size as a knob.
3. **Budget-allocation comparison** under matched information and compute, including the control the review identified: infer the specification once and hand it to the agent.
4. **Exploitation analysis**: where learned-verifier errors concentrate and how search finds them, measured against executable ground truth.

## 4. Hypotheses (revised)

| # | Hypothesis | Status | Measurement |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | **Allocation.** Verifier-first search outperforms (a) handing the inferred specification directly to the agent and (b) updating the agent directly on expert-labeled responses, *for some budgets and not others*; the paper maps where. Universal dominance is not claimed. | Central | Independently measured correctness vs expert seconds, three arms, identical rules, rationales and compute |
| H2 | **Representation of the learned spec.** Checklist conditions with provenance reach usable agreement with fewer labels than retrieved exemplars or holistic rubric prose, and are exploited less under optimization. Controls: prompt length, compute, provenance. | Central | κ vs labels; exploitation gap vs optimization steps; three verifier representations; Learnable Assessment Skills and SibylSense rubric construction as baselines |
| H3 | **Error concentration × optimization pressure.** Search exploits the learned verifier where its errors concentrate (by rule, by bucket); the realized exploitation gap grows with the optimizer's behavioral divergence from reference. Replaces the earlier "κ threshold / predicted flip point", which the bound does not support. | Central | Verifier error map (per rule / bucket, vs executable truth) before optimization; exploitation gap after; divergence measured behaviorally |
| H4 | **Provenance.** Tiered memory with locked label-derived checks holds agreement under injected agent hypotheses; flat memory drifts. | Secondary ablation vs Rulers-style locking | κ over epochs, with/without tiers, with hypothesis injection |
| H5 | Representation freedom / reliability-rewarded structure | **Deferred** — separate paper if ever | — |

Secondary measurements, reported without hypotheses: κ₀ (zero-label verifier) vs history size; exchange rate between channels at matched κ (`MATH.md` §1.4); stand-in coverage at bounded risk using a sound selective-risk procedure (`MATH.md` §3), with its sample-size cost stated.

## 5. Evaluation protocol (the part the review said was broken)

### 5.1 Ground truth is executable, not an LLM

Hidden policies are implemented as executable predicates over `(request, state, response)`. Correctness of any response is computed, not judged. The policy-aware LLM "oracle" is only a *noisy query channel* for methods; it is never the grader.

### 5.2 Four disjoint data roles

| Role | Contents | Who touches it |
|---|---|---|
| **Learning** | histories; oracle answers to signal-driven and random-curation queries | rubric curation, policy updates, selection |
| **Search validation** | the gate's paired-comparison set | gate only; it *is* a search-validation set and is reported as such, never as test |
| **Calibration** | oracle answers to random-calibration queries, drawn from the *current* policy's outputs | κ, Brier, exploitation gap, selective-risk thresholds; never curation |
| **Final test** | held-out cases *and held-out policy instances* | touched once, after the system is frozen |

The old "sealed" set is renamed search validation. The random audit slice is split into random-curation and random-calibration, so no answer serves two roles.

### 5.3 Reference-free is the principal regime

Offline, the verifier can see the historical response. In deployment it cannot. All headline numbers are computed in the reference-free regime: the verifier scores fresh candidate responses to requests with no reference, and correctness is computed from the executable policy. Reference-aided numbers are reported as a secondary condition.

### 5.4 Recertification

Optimization changes the distribution of candidate responses. Every calibration quantity is re-estimated from the current policy's outputs after each accepted change; nothing certified on AOP_t is carried to AOP_{t+1}.

## 6. Baselines and controls

| Arm | Why it is required |
|---|---|
| Long-context procedure writing: all histories in context → write the procedure → execute | Kill test; if it solves the hard splits the thesis is wrong |
| Retrieval few-shot: nearest histories at inference, no explicit procedure | Is an explicit procedure needed at all |
| **Spec-once-then-policy**: infer the specification once (same histories, same expert answers), hand it to the agent in context, no verifier loop | **The control the paper lives or dies on.** If it matches verifier-first, the verifier is overhead |
| Policy-first: update the agent directly on expert-labeled responses | H1 arm (b) |
| True-spec-revealed: the agent given the hidden policy | Upper bound; separates missing requirements from execution limits |
| Terminal-reward harness evolution | Isolates the value of state-verifiable facts |
| SibylSense-style alternating rubric memory + policy update | Closest prior method |
| Learnable Assessment Skills rubric construction | H2 baseline |
| Holistic judge / exemplar judge | H2 comparators |
| Flat memory | H4 comparator |

All arms receive identical histories, identical expert answers (same questions, same rationales), identical expert seconds, and matched compute. Expert seconds are the budget unit, not label count, because a rule confirmation and a response verdict cost different amounts of time.

## 9. The two-week pilot (X0) — runs first

1. Build the small confirmations environment with **executable hidden rules** and **three policy instances** (`../benchmark/README.md`).
2. **True-spec-revealed** run: the agent given each hidden policy. Establishes the execution ceiling and separates missing requirements from execution limits.
3. Five arms, matched on histories, expert answers, expert seconds, and compute: long-context induction; retrieval few-shot; spec-once-then-policy; policy-first; verifier-first.
4. For the learned-verifier arms, optimize against the verifier and evaluate *fresh* outputs against the executable specification, reference-free.
5. Report: correctness vs expert seconds per arm; verifier error map; exploitation gap after optimization.

**Continue** if verifier-first shows a reproducible expert-time advantage over spec-once-then-policy at some budget, or a useful, explainable reliability boundary. **Revise the method claim** if its advantage disappears under equal information and compute — the benchmark and the allocation map are still a paper, but a different one.

Cost: tens of dollars of API calls for the long-context arm; the verifier-first arm is the expensive one and is run at reduced k and epochs in the pilot.

## 11. What would make it a weaker paper, honestly

- The kill test passes: boundary result plus benchmark.
- Spec-once-then-policy matches verifier-first everywhere: the paper becomes the allocation map and benchmark, with verifier-first as a negative result. Still publishable; the method claim is dropped.
- Firm data unavailable: X8 dropped; the external-validity paragraph is lost.
- Results mushy: workshop paper with a clean benchmark.
- SibylSense-style baseline matches or beats verifier-first: report it; the contribution narrows to the setting, the benchmark, and the exploitation analysis.
